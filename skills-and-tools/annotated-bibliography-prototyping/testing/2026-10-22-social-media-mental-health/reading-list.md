This reading list combines social comparison, self-esteem, FOMO, sleep, and broader context. It includes nine abstract-level candidates; Garett et al. (2016) is a deliberate exception to the preferred 2018-2026 date range because it directly studies undergraduate social-media timing and sleep quality.

## Comparison, feedback, and self-esteem work through different pathways

**Citation:** Krause, H.-V., Baum, K., Baumann, A., & Krasnova, H. (2019). Unifying the detrimental and beneficial effects of social network site use on self-esteem: A systematic literature review. *Media Psychology, 24*(1), 10-47. https://doi.org/10.1080/15213269.2019.1656646  
**Source ID:** `component-10`  
**Source Type:** Systematic literature review  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence  
**Study Design:** Systematic literature review  
**Evidence Base:** Systematic literature review; abstract reports a framework but not a full count of included studies.  
**Credibility:** Peer-reviewed venue; theoretical framework and systematic review, with individual-study quality not visible in the abstract.  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** Medium

The review directly distinguishes harmful comparison from beneficial feedback or self-reflection, helping the student separate total use from specific activity.

### Questions this source addresses

- How does social comparison change self-esteem on SNSs?
- When does positive social feedback or self-reflection help self-esteem?

### Key quotes

- "when a social networking site is used to compare oneself with others, it mostly results in decreases in users' self-esteem"
- "receiving positive social feedback from others or using these platforms to reflect on one's own self is mainly associated with benefits for users' self-esteem"

---
## Comparison and envy are linked to depression, but direction is uncertain

**Citation:** Carraturo, F., Di Perna, T., Giannicola, V., Nacchia, M. A., Pepe, M., Muzii, B., Bottone, M., Sperandeo, R., Bochicchio, V., Maldonato, N. M., & Scandurra, C. (2023). Envy, social comparison, and depression on social networking sites: A systematic review. *European Journal of Investigation in Health, Psychology and Education, 13*(2), 364-376. https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe13020027  
**Source ID:** `landscape-19`  
**Source Type:** Systematic review article  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence  
**Study Design:** Systematic review  
**Evidence Base:** 9 studies meeting inclusion criteria from PsycINFO, PubMed, and Web of Science, 2012-2022.  
**Credibility:** Peer-reviewed venue; small included-study count and limited directional evidence.  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** Medium

This systematic review directly maps the comparison-envy-depression branch and cautions that direction is uncertain.

### Questions this source addresses

- How are social comparison, envy, and depression related on SNSs?
- What is the direction of that relationship in studies able to test it?

### Key quotes

- "In all articles reviewed, a simple correlation was found between SC on SNSs, envy, and depression"
- "The only two studies that were suitable to determine direction found that depression acted as a predictor rather than an outcome of SC and envy"

---
## Self-esteem and FOMO may mediate the SNS-depression pathway

**Citation:** Leung, A. N. M., Law, W., Liang, Y. Y., Au, A. C. L., Li, C., & Ng, H. K. S. (2021). What explains the association between usage of social networking sites (SNS) and depression symptoms? The mediating roles of self-esteem and fear of missing out. *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 18*(8), 3916. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18083916  
**Source ID:** `component-03`  
**Source Type:** Research article  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence  
**Study Design:** Three studies of Chinese college/university students  
**Evidence Base:** Studies 1-3 included 347, 180, and 233 Chinese college/university students respectively.  
**Credibility:** Peer-reviewed venue; three-study sequence strengthens internal consistency but abstract-level access limits full-method review.  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** Medium

This source directly tests two pathways named in the student's interests: self-esteem and FOMO.

### Questions this source addresses

- Do self-esteem and FOMO mediate the association between SNS time and depression?
- What usage thresholds appeared associated with subthreshold depression?

### Key quotes

- "Results showed that more time spent on SNS was related to lower self-esteem, and higher FoMO, respectively; while self-esteem then negatively, and FoMO then positively, explained depression symptoms, respectively"
- "Combining results from three studies, both self-esteem and FoMO mediated the relation between SNS usage and depression symptoms"

## Impression management and comparison correlate with depression and anxiety

**Citation:** Ojha, K., Soohinda, G., Sampath, H., & Dutta, S. (2021). Social networking sites and its relation to social comparison and psychological well-being among medical university students. *Indian Journal of Psychiatry, 63*(6), 593-596. https://doi.org/10.4103/indianjpsychiatry.indianjpsychiatry_1344_20  
**Source ID:** `landscape-11`  
**Source Type:** Research article  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence with population caveat  
**Study Design:** Cross-sectional questionnaire study  
**Evidence Base:** 220 medical students in India; mean age 20.44 years.  
**Credibility:** Peer-reviewed venue; cross-sectional design limits causal inference.  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** Medium

This student-sample study connects impression management, social comparison, depression, and anxiety, though it focuses on medical students rather than college students broadly.

### Questions this source addresses

- How are impression management, social comparison, depression, and anxiety related?
- Does SNS duration show a complex association with psychopathology?

### Key quotes

- "Impression management was associated with higher social comparison, depression, and anxiety scores"
- "Social comparison had a significant correlation between depression and anxiety scores"

## Limiting use reduced loneliness and depression in an experiment

**Citation:** Hunt, M. G., Marx, R., Lipson, C., & Young, J. (2018). No more FOMO: Limiting social media decreases loneliness and depression. *Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 37*(10), 751-768. https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751  
**Source ID:** `landscape-15`  
**Source Type:** Experimental research article  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence  
**Study Design:** Randomized controlled experiment  
**Evidence Base:** 143 undergraduates at the University of Pennsylvania randomized to limited use or usual use for three weeks.  
**Credibility:** Peer-reviewed venue; randomized experimental design, but abstract-level access limits full-method review.  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** High

This is the strongest source in the set for testing whether limiting use affects well-being rather than merely correlating with it.

### Questions this source addresses

- Does limiting Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat improve loneliness and depression?
- Does self-monitoring affect anxiety and FOMO?

### Key quotes

- "The limited use group showed significant reductions in loneliness and depression over three weeks compared to the control group"
- "Our findings strongly suggest that limiting social media use to approximately 30 minutes per day may lead to significant improvement in well-being"

---
## Compensatory use and family support follow different pathways

**Citation:** Zhou, M., Li, F., Wang, Y., Chen, S., & Wang, K. (2020). Compensatory social networking site use, family support, and depression among college freshman: Three-wave panel study. *Journal of Medical Internet Research, 22*(9), e18458. https://doi.org/10.2196/18458  
**Source ID:** `landscape-14`  
**Source Type:** Research article  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence  
**Study Design:** Three-wave panel study  
**Evidence Base:** Three-wave panel of 1,137 college freshmen.  
**Credibility:** Peer-reviewed venue; longitudinal panel design strengthens temporal inference.  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** Medium

This longitudinal study adds a college-transition pathway and shows that social context can alter how compensatory use operates.

### Questions this source addresses

- How do introversion, compensatory SNS use, family support, and depression relate over time?
- Does family support buffer or alter the pathway differently by introversion level?

### Key quotes

- "introversion at Wave 1 positively predicted compensatory use of SNS at Wave 2 and subsequently increased depression at Wave 3"
- "Unexpectedly, we found that family support in Wave 1 decreased compensatory SNS use for less introverted freshmen in Wave 2 and further decreased depression in Wave 3"

---
## Problematic use broadens the mental-health and sleep picture

**Citation:** Weinstein, A. M. (2023). Problematic social networking site use-effects on mental health and the brain. *Frontiers in Psychiatry, 13*, Article 1106004. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.1106004  
**Source ID:** `landscape-05`  
**Source Type:** Review article  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence; review synthesis  
**Study Design:** Review  
**Evidence Base:** Narrative review of evidence on problematic SNS use; includes limited brain-imaging evidence and preliminary CBT evidence.  
**Credibility:** Peer-reviewed venue; review is described as broad but not systematic in the abstract.  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** Medium

This review broadens the landscape beyond depression and anxiety and identifies sleep as one recurring pathway.

### Questions this source addresses

- Which mental-health and sleep outcomes are associated with problematic SNS use?
- What preliminary treatment evidence exists?

### Key quotes

- "Problematic SNS use has been associated with an increased rate of depression, anxiety, stress, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and propensity to excessive alcohol use"
- "There is evidence for reduced sleep quality and quantity, longer sleeping latency and more sleep disturbance"

---
## Timing of use matters for sleep quality

**Citation:** Garett, R., Liu, S., & Young, S. D. (2016). The relationship between social media use and sleep quality among undergraduate students. *Information, Communication & Society, 21*(2), 163-173. https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2016.1266374  
**Source ID:** `sleep-02`  
**Source Type:** Research article  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence with date caveat  
**Study Design:** Longitudinal undergraduate study with tweet content and weekly surveys  
**Evidence Base:** 197 Twitter-using first-quarter freshmen; tweet content and weekly sleep surveys.  
**Credibility:** Peer-reviewed venue; direct undergraduate data, but older than preferred date range and focused on Twitter.  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** Medium

Although older than the preferred 2018-2026 range, this source directly studies undergraduates and distinguishes late-night and weekday-evening timing effects.

### Questions this source addresses

- How is the timing of social-media posting associated with sleep quality?
- Are fear-related posts associated with lower sleep quality?

### Key quotes

- "Tweeting more frequently on weekday late nights was associated with lower sleep quality"
- "tweeting more frequently on weekday evenings was associated with better quality sleep"

---
## Problematic use aligns with poor sleep and multiple symptoms

**Citation:** Hussain, Z., & Griffiths, M. D. (2019). The associations between problematic social networking site use and sleep quality, attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, depression, anxiety and stress. *International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction, 19*(3), 686-700. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11469-019-00175-1  
**Source ID:** `sleep-04`  
**Source Type:** Research article  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence with population caveat  
**Study Design:** Cross-sectional online survey  
**Evidence Base:** 638 SNS users; mean age 32.03 years, SD 10.08.  
**Credibility:** Peer-reviewed venue; cross-sectional design and broader age range limit fit to the undergraduate target.  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** Medium

This study links problematic SNS use with sleep and several mental-health symptoms, though its sample is broader and older on average than the target undergraduate range.

### Questions this source addresses

- Is problematic SNS use associated with poor sleep quality?
- How does problematic use correlate with ADHD, depression, anxiety, and stress?

### Key quotes

- "Bivariate correlations identified moderate positive correlations between PSNSU, poor sleep quality"
- "The study demonstrated that PSNSU was associated with psychopathological and psychiatric disorders symptoms"
