# Reading List: Capstone Course Design and Professional Preparation

This is a starting list for understanding how capstone courses can be structured and how they connect to professional readiness. It prioritizes sources that describe concrete design elements, student experience, and career-related learning outcomes.

## Iterative design thinking as a capstone scaffold

**Citation:** Brandão, A. P., Donaldson, J. P., Dunlap, K. A., Wiegert, J. G., Kao, S., & Paudyal, S. (2024). Design thinking for engaged learning in animal science: Lessons from five semesters of a senior capstone course. *Translational Animal Science*. https://doi.org/10.1093/tas/txae020  
**Source ID:** `landscape-01`  
**Source Type:** Article  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence  
**Study Design:** Design-based research across five course iterations  
**Evidence Base:** Student reflections from 276 participants across five semesters; network analysis of strengths, struggles, and theory alignment  
**Credibility:** Peer-reviewed journal article; retrieved abstract describes an iterative study and explicit design principles  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** High

The source is useful if you want a concrete model for staging capstone work and revising it between iterations. It explicitly connects design thinking to collaborative project-based learning and student reflection.

### Questions this source addresses

- How does scaffolded design thinking shape capstone engagement and teamwork?
- How can student feedback be used to revise a capstone course?

### Key quotes

- “DTEL scaffolds design thinking into 10 stages for collaborative project-based learning to foster skills like problem solving and teamwork.”
- “Continuous refinement based on student feedback is key to creating effective learning experiences that align with principles from learning theories.”

---

## Stakeholder incentives in engineering capstones

**Citation:** Blackwell, R. Q., Hammad, E., Jin, C., Park, J., & Patterson, A. E. (2026). Modeling conflicting incentives in engineering senior capstone projects: A multi-player game theory approach. *International Journal of Mechanical Engineering Education*. https://doi.org/10.1177/03064190261429065  
**Source ID:** `landscape-03`  
**Source Type:** Article  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence  
**Study Design:** Game-theoretic modeling  
**Evidence Base:** Formal model of interactions among students, faculty, and industry sponsors  
**Credibility:** Peer-reviewed journal article; retrieved abstract describes a formal framework  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** High

This source is useful for thinking about why capstone projects can become difficult even when participants are motivated. It frames stakeholder misalignment as a structural problem rather than a simple communication failure.

### Questions this source addresses

- How can conflicting incentives among students, faculty, and sponsors be modeled?
- What strategic dependencies arise in sponsored capstone projects?

### Key quotes

- “University engineering capstone projects involve sustained interaction among students, faculty, and industry sponsors whose objectives are only partially aligned.”
- “Existing analyses typically treat stakeholder behavior informally or descriptively.”

---

## Systematic design of a construction-management capstone

**Citation:** Lee, N., & Kim, S. J. (2020). A systematic course design approach to guide the development of a construction engineering and management capstone course. In *2020 Annual Conference Northeast Section (ASEE-NE)*. IEEE. https://doi.org/10.1109/ASEENE51624.2020.9292652  
**Source ID:** `landscape-09`  
**Source Type:** Conference proceeding  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence  
**Study Design:** Conference paper on systematic course design  
**Evidence Base:** Course-design case for a construction engineering and management capstone  
**Credibility:** Peer-reviewed conference proceedings; retrieved abstract describes the course-design purpose  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** Medium

This source is useful if you are translating a disciplinary capstone into an explicit course design. It connects problem-solving and critical-thinking goals to the design of the capstone course itself.

### Questions this source addresses

- How do different capstone course designs prepare students for professional practice?
- How can a disciplinary capstone be systematically designed?

### Key quotes

- “A Senior Capstone course is a hallmark of most undergraduate construction engineering and management (CEM) programs.”
- “Many undergraduate CEM programs implement their own capstone course within their curricula to emphasize preparing students for the realities of the construction industry.”

## Learning-centered preparation for capstone work

**Citation:** Webster, R. (n.d.). A learning-centered paradigm for engineering graphics and design: Engineering technology students’ skill gains and capstone preparation.  
**Source ID:** `landscape-10`  
**Source Type:** Article  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence  
**Study Design:** Descriptive non-experimental follow-on study  
**Evidence Base:** Engineering technology students; retrieved abstract describes skill gains and capstone preparation  
**Credibility:** Scholarly journal article; small-sample study; limitations not explicit in retrieved abstract  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** Medium

This source is useful for thinking about how earlier coursework can prepare students for capstone expectations. It focuses on active learning and professional or technical skill development rather than capstone delivery alone.

### Questions this source addresses

- How do earlier learning experiences prepare students for capstone work?
- What skill gains do students report before entering capstone?

### Key quotes

- “This descriptive non-experimental followon study investigates the extent to which a learning-centered paradigm in an engineering graphics and design course prepared undergraduate engineering technology (ET) students for senior capstone.”
- “The course, which was approximately 5% passive and 95% active learning, produced positive skill gains for ET students.”

## Career information literacy in STEM capstone units

**Citation:** Lin-Stephens, S., Manuguerra, M., & Uesi, J. (2018). Comparing STEM students’ and employers’ emphases on career information literacy: A study on undergraduate capstone units. *International Journal of Innovation in Science and Mathematics Education*.  
**Source ID:** `component-01`  
**Source Type:** Article  
**Connection Type:** Direct evidence  
**Study Design:** Nonexperimental, cross-sectional comparison  
**Evidence Base:** Final-year STEM capstone students and potential employers at an Australian university  
**Credibility:** Scholarly journal article; no DOI located in the retrieved record; limitations are acknowledged in the abstract  
**Access Level:** Abstract only  
**Confidence:** Medium

This source is useful when capstone design is intended to support employability. It compares what students and employers emphasize and points to a gap in career-related information literacy.

### Questions this source addresses

- How do student and employer emphases on career information literacy align?
- What career-related learning should capstone courses address?

### Key quotes

- “The results point to a critical need for STEM students to be educated about employer perceptions.”
- “The STEM students exhibit significantly different career information literacy focuses from STEM employers.”
