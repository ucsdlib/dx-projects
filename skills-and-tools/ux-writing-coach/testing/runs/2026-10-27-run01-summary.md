## Cross-Scenario Findings

### What worked consistently

- **Molecule use:** Most responses selected and adapted the correct content molecule. The service overview, offering cards, status message, contact-and-support structure, and boundary content were all recognizable and task-first.
- **User-centered structure:** The coach consistently put the user’s action or benefit before process details, eligibility, limits, and internal context.
- **Follow-up handling:** Follow-up turns refined or condensed the prior output rather than restarting the analysis.
- **Accuracy discipline:** No scenario invented a service, link, deadline, restoration time, location, policy, or staff name.
- **Review framing:** Every draft ended with a concrete next step for human review, and the more material scenarios also identified accessibility or user-testing checks.

### Systemic issues

1. **Verification reminders drift.** The coach often identifies unverified facts in one turn but does not repeat them in a later revision. A1 did not restate contact and link checks, A4 did not repeat deadline and submission-link checks in Turn 3, and A5 did not repeat hours and links in Turn 3.
2. **Audience and eligibility terms need stricter handling.** A2 changed “University affiliates” to “UC San Diego affiliates” without flagging that as a material terminology decision. A6 appropriately asked whether “other affiliations” and “Library front desk” matched approved public wording.
3. **Style rules do not always govern formatting.** A2 used em dashes in both suggested copy and list formatting despite the skill’s explicit instruction to avoid them.
4. **Calls to action can still be generic.** “Start a request” and “Learn about onsite use” appeared in A2. They are better than “Submit” or unqualified “Learn more,” but they do not name the task precisely enough.
5. **Internal context can leak into first drafts.** A1 published the under-five-minute expectation before recommending removal in the follow-up. Service metrics should be treated as internal source material from the first draft.

## Style Alignment Evaluation

### Overall assessment

The skill wrote copy that is broadly aligned with `references/voice-and-messaging.md`, `references/style-mechanics.md`, and `references/ux-writing-principles.md`. Its strongest work was user-centered, calm, and task-first, especially in service-critical scenarios. It also avoided the most serious naming errors: no scenario used “UCSD,” and the responses generally preserved material limits without inventing facts.

Alignment was less consistent at the mechanical layer. The coach understood the style rules but did not always apply them to its own formatting and suggested options. In particular, A2 and A4 used em dashes in suggested copy, and some skill-output labels used title case even when the underlying page copy was better aligned with sentence case. These are not publication blockers, but they show that style guidance is not yet consistently enforced at the output layer.

### Highest-value style improvements

1. Enforce the em-dash rule across suggested copy, option labels, bullets, and formatting, not only prose.
2. Add a sentence-case checkpoint for page-facing headings and labels, while allowing proper nouns and formal service names to retain capitalization.
3. Add a compact jargon-handling cue that asks whether “known-item search,” “other affiliations,” “non-circulating,” and “registered Community Borrowers” are recognizable to the intended first-time user.
4. Make accessibility-adjacent checks routine for any output that produces page headings, cards, alerts, or contact pathways.
5. Keep internal metrics, workflow labels, and role names out of first drafts, as seen in A1’s initial use of the under-five-minute expectation.

## Options, Iteration, and Self-Identification

### Self-identification

The tested skill did not instruct the coach to identify itself, and no run opened with a skill name or capability statement. That is a skill-level gap rather than a model noncompliance issue. `SKILL.md` now adds a one-line first-response introduction so future sessions make the coaching mode visible without disrupting the request.

### Interpretation

The skill is strongest when the request is clearly microcopy or short-form. In those cases, it often produces distinct options and explains the tradeoff. It is less consistent for long-form or structured page content, where it often gives one polished revision even when an alternative section treatment would help the user compare emphasis, tone, or length.

Next steps are a clear strength. Most turns identify a human reviewer, verification task, accessibility check, or user-testing need. The weaker part of the process is explicit iteration: the coach often implies that the user can return with feedback, but it rarely asks which option to develop or what constraint to apply next. That is worth correcting in the skill.

## Improvement Recommendations

1. Add a compact service-content verification checklist that persists across revision turns: approved public name, locations, hours, contact methods, response expectations, links, deadlines, eligibility, and limits.
2. Require material changes to supplied eligibility or audience terms to be labeled as proposed terminology changes requiring service-owner verification.
3. Apply the em-dash prohibition to suggested copy, bullets, rationales, and formatting, not only sentence prose.
4. Add stricter call-to-action guidance that names the task, object, or destination whenever “request,” “learn,” “find,” or “start” could be ambiguous.
5. Add an internal-content filter for service metrics, workflow labels, staffing, routing, and role names before producing a first draft.
6. Add a short audience-fit hypothesis cue for evaluation requests and recommend user testing when audience vocabulary or task fit is uncertain.
7. Provide an ultra-short alert pattern for status messages where the known facts are outage, support path, alternatives, and unknown restoration time.
8. Add explicit long-form guidance to offer alternative treatments of the highest-impact section rather than only one polished rewrite.
9. Add a first-response identification line so the user knows when the UX Writing Coach is active.
10. Use the new `UXWC_TEST_MODE=1` marker in future tests so reference-use findings are not limited to conceptual alignment.
