# Inclusive Language

Use this reference when copy refers to people, identity, disability, gender, race, ethnicity, age, nationality, religion, or similar characteristics. Include identity only when relevant to the communication or task.

## Core Principles

- Avoid irrelevant references to personal characteristics.
- Use terminology currently accepted by the individual or community. When in doubt, ask the person how they want to be identified.
- Prefer language that centers the user’s task and agency rather than a deficit or barrier.
- Be specific rather than broad. For example, name a tribe, country, program, or accommodation need only when relevant and accurate.
- If a term is not in these notes, verify it with the user or the UC San Diego Editorial Style Guide before using it.

## Gender and Pronouns

- Gender is internal and social identity; sex is biological and not always binary or fixed. Avoid “both,” “either,” or “opposite” sex or gender when that framing excludes nonbinary and intersex people.
- Prefer “woman” or “women” over “female” as an adjective when referring to people.
- Use gender-neutral phrasing where appropriate.
- Always defer to a person’s stated pronouns. If pronouns are unknown and necessary, ask the writer to confirm rather than assume.
- Use singular “they/them/their” for an unspecified or unknown person: “A student can pick up their holds.”
- Use “they” rather than “he/she” or “he or she” for indefinite subjects.
- Do not assume pronouns from a name or appearance. Do not assume gender identity from pronouns.
- Use “personal pronouns,” not “preferred” or “chosen” pronouns.
- Avoid neopronouns such as “xe” or “zim” for general audiences unless the person uses them and asks to be identified that way.

## Disability

- Ask people how they want to be described when possible. Both person-first and identity-first language may be appropriate depending on individual preference.
- “Disabilities” and “disabled” are generally acceptable when relevant. Do not use euphemisms unless quoting or explaining how an individual describes themself.
- Do not use “handicap” for a disability or “handicapped” for a person.
- Describe accommodations and access features plainly: “wheelchair-accessible entrance,” “screen-reader compatible,” “sensory-friendly hours.”
- Avoid mentioning a disability unless it is relevant to the task or service.

## Race and Ethnicity

- Follow the UC San Diego guide and AP with awareness that language evolves.
- Be specific whenever possible. Name a country, tribe, community, or program only when relevant and confirmed.
- If you do not know how someone identifies, ask.
- Capitalize **Black** as an adjective in racial, ethnic, or cultural contexts; do not use it as a singular or plural noun.
- Lowercase **white** as an adjective in racial, ethnic, or cultural contexts.
- Avoid broad or imprecise shorthand such as “brown” unless directly quoted.
- Use “Indigenous people” or “Indigenous peoples” capitalized in reference to original inhabitants of a place.
- For Native Americans, specify tribal affiliation when possible.
- Avoid using “Asian” as shorthand for “Asian American” when a more specific term is appropriate.
- Avoid “Caucasian” as a synonym for white unless directly quoted.

## Service and Assistance Animals

- Use “service animal” for animals trained to perform tasks for a person with a disability under the Americans with Disabilities Act.
- Do not describe emotional support or therapy animals as service animals. If relevant, use the specific term the policy or service uses.

## Age, Weight, Appearance, Nationality, Religion, and Social Standing

- Avoid irrelevant references to these characteristics.
- If one is relevant, use the person’s or community’s preferred language and avoid value judgments.
- For age, use numerals: “6-year-old student.”
- For nationality or origin, be specific and respectful.
- For religion, use the person’s or organization’s preferred spelling and capitalization.

## Inclusive UX Patterns

| Avoid | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| “Handicapped restroom” | “Accessible restroom” |
| “Suffers from anxiety” | “Student with anxiety” or “Student who has anxiety,” if relevant |
| “His or her holds” | “Their holds” |
| “Female students” | “Women students,” if gender is relevant |
| “Non-traditional student” | “Transfer student,” “returning student,” or another precise term |
| “Exotic cultures” | Name the culture, tradition, or community |
| “The disabled” | “Disabled people” or “people with disabilities,” following preference |

When uncertainty could affect dignity, policy, or legal status, stop and ask the writer to confirm with the person, office, or authoritative campus source.
