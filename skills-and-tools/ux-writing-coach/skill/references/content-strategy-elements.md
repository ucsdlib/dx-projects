# Content Strategy Elements and Molecules

Use this model to plan Library service copy from the smallest useful element to a reusable molecule. An **element** is a single piece of copy or supporting information, such as a title, lead, availability note, or button label. A **molecule** is a recurring composition of elements, such as a call to action or service-offering card. Molecules can become page sections and templates, but start with the user’s task before choosing a layout.

Service definitions are internal source material. Translate them into public-facing copy; do not paste internal scope, staffing, metrics, or process language onto a page. Verify the public service name, eligibility, locations, hours, deadlines, links, and policies before drafting.

## Content Elements

| Element | Purpose and guidance |
| --- | --- |
| Title | Name the service or section in the user’s terms. Use the approved public-facing name whenever the service is mentioned. Prefer user-centered verbs when the title describes an action, such as “Request instruction” rather than “Library instructional services.” Aim for less than 50 characters when practical. |
| Lead | Motivate and orient the user in one or two sentences. State the user’s main action or benefit first, then the essential context. Avoid restating the title. |
| Description | Explain the service’s value and impact in one or two short paragraphs. Answer what the service makes possible and what the user can do or become. Lead with outcomes, not internal workflow. |
| Offering | Describe one distinct way to use the service. Give the offering a task-first title, a one- or two-sentence summary, and a next step. Distinguish offerings by user outcome or modality, not by internal unit structure. |
| Call to action | Use a specific verb phrase for the immediate result, such as “Request this item,” “Reserve a room,” or “Submit your nomination.” Avoid generic “Submit,” “Click here,” and unqualified “Learn more.” When a verb such as “request,” “learn,” “find,” or “start” could be ambiguous, name the task, object, or destination. |
| Link | Use descriptive text that makes sense out of context. Name the destination, resource, or task. Avoid bare URLs and “click here.” |
| Eligibility note | Say who can take the action after the user sees the possible action. Use audience categories the user recognizes, such as “UC San Diego students” or “registered community borrowers.” Preserve all material limits and do not imply eligibility that does not exist. |
| Requirement | Name credentials, registration, reservations, equipment, forms, or approval needed to complete the task. Put requirements after the opening action unless they change whether the user should proceed. |
| Limit or boundary | State checkout periods, quantities, turnaround, fees, restrictions, and exclusions in concrete terms. Where possible, pair the boundary with an alternative or next step. |
| Availability or status | Say whether a resource, space, staffed service, or technology is available, unavailable, delayed, or limited. Include the current condition and next action without blaming the user. |
| Location | Use the exact building, floor, service point, room, or collection location. Add a direction or nearby landmark when the formal name alone is not enough. Do not say “the Library” when the user needs a specific destination. |
| Hours and timing | Include the date, day, hours, deadline, turnaround, or service window needed to act. Use campus style for dates and times. Include a time zone only when the audience spans zones. |
| Contact or help path | Tell users where to get help for this task. Use the most specific accurate pathway first, such as a form, email, phone line, consultation, or service desk. A general “Contact Us” path is appropriate only when no more specific path exists. |
| Policy link or summary | Give the rule needed to act in plain language, then link to the full policy for detail. Use policy names consistently. Do not summarize in a way that hides a material limit. |
| Image | Use an image only when it helps users recognize a place, service, resource, or outcome. Include descriptive alt text and a caption when the image adds meaning that body copy does not already provide. |
| Icon label | Pair an icon with a clear text label. Do not use an icon as the sole carrier of meaning. Use the accessible text needed for the icon’s purpose; TDX will provide the approved icon library. |
| Confirmation or result | Tell users what happened and what comes next. Include any expected timeframe, account or form to check, and a help path when appropriate. |
| Related service or resource | Point to the next most relevant task or service, not every related page. Describe the destination and distinguish it from the current service. |

## Service Definition to Copy Map

| Service definition section | Likely public copy elements | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title or service name | Title | Verify the approved public-facing name; internal and public names may differ. |
| Description or vision | Lead and description | Translate institutional value into the user’s task and benefit. |
| Service offerings | Offering title, summary, call to action, availability, eligibility | Write each offering from the user’s outcome, not the delivery structure. |
| Scope and boundaries | Eligibility note, requirement, limit or boundary, alternative, policy link | Do not publish internal in/out-of-scope lists wholesale. |
| Audiences and affiliation types | Eligibility note, contact or help path, audience-specific variation | Avoid labels users may not recognize; define campus terms when needed. |
| Service calendar | Hours and timing, availability or status, event details | Keep internal workflow dates separate from user-facing deadlines and hours. |
| Roles and staffing | Contact or help path | Name teams or service points only when useful; do not expose internal routing details. |
| Metrics and measures of success | Usually none | Use metrics to improve service copy, not as public page copy unless they are user-meaningful. |
| Related documents and pages | Link, related service or resource | Curate only links that help the user’s next step. |

## Choosing a Molecule

Choose the molecule from the user’s jobs, audience, and placement, not from the order of sentences in the source material.

| User need | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| One primary action and its essential context | Service hero |
| Understand a service and choose among pathways | Service overview |
| Compare distinct tasks at a glance | Service-offering cards |
| Know whether they are allowed to act | Eligibility and access |
| Complete a sequence | How-to path |
| Recover from a problem or system state | Status, alert, or help message |
| Get task-specific help or expertise | Contact and support |
| Find or use a collection item | Catalog and resource action |
| Locate a space | Location and wayfinding |

If the user requests a specific format, use it when it fits the task. If it does not, say why and recommend a better structure. Offer alternatives only when a real tradeoff exists; do not ask non-designers to choose among abstract layout labels.

## Copy Molecules

### Call to action

**Composition:** Title + Lead + Button or link.

Use this molecule when a user needs one clear action. The title names the task, the lead states the immediate benefit or context, and the button or link describes the result.

Example:

- **Title:** Borrow books and media
- **Lead:** Check out circulating materials with your Library card.
- **Button:** Find borrowing options

Keep the button and title consistent but not repetitive. If eligibility or timing affects the action, add an eligibility note or hours and timing.

### Service hero

**Composition:** Title + Lead + Primary call to action + Contact or help path.

Use at the top of a service page to answer “What can I do here?” in seconds. The lead should carry the service’s most important user outcome. The primary call to action should reflect the most common or highest-priority task, not the easiest task to automate.

Add availability or status when service hours, access, outages, or turnaround could change the user’s decision. Add eligibility only when it is essential or place it in the next section.

### Service overview

**Composition:** Lead + Description + Service offerings + Contact or help path.

Use this molecule for a page that introduces one Library service with multiple pathways. Let the lead and description establish the common purpose, then present each offering as a task-first unit. Keep offering summaries parallel in structure and similar in length.

For a large service, publish a page or section for each offering and use this overview to route users rather than explaining every workflow on one page.

### Service-offering card

**Composition:** Offering title + Summary + Primary call to action + Availability, eligibility, or location as needed.

Use for self-service and mediated options, such as paging and delivery, course reserves, borrower account management, self-service checkout, consultations, workshops, or technology lending. The title should describe what the user can do; the summary should distinguish this option from the others.

Example:

- **Title:** Pick up your holds
- **Summary:** Requested items are held for pickup at the location you selected.
- **Button:** See pickup locations

### Eligibility and access

**Composition:** Task headline + Lead with allowed action + Eligibility note + Requirement or limit + Policy link or alternative.

Use when affiliation, registration, credentials, fees, reservations, or borrowing privileges affect the task. Lead with the possible action, then make the boundary plain.

Example:

- **Borrow physical collections.** UC San Diego affiliates and registered community borrowers can check out eligible materials. Community borrowers can register at a Library service desk. Some items are Library use only.

Avoid beginning with “Access restricted,” “Not available,” or an internal policy label. Say what is possible, who can do it, and what alternative exists.

### How-to path

**Composition:** Task title + Lead + Numbered steps or short explanation + Confirmation or result + Contact or help path.

Use for workflows with a sequence, such as requesting materials, registering as a community borrower, submitting a prize entry, reserving equipment, or connecting to Library Wi-Fi. Keep each step to one action. Put preparatory requirements before the first action and exceptions after the main path.

If the process is two steps or fewer, a short paragraph or bulleted summary may serve users better than a numbered list.

### Status, alert, or help message

**Composition:** Plain-language status + Explanation if useful + Next action + Alternative or support path.

Use for outages, access errors, delayed requests, unavailable rooms or equipment, and service closures. Say what happened, what the user can do now, and where to get help. Keep the tone calm and concrete; preserve the material facts.

Example:

- **Your request did not go through.** Your Library account may need renewal. Renew your account or contact the Geisel Library Front Desk for help.

### Contact and support

**Composition:** Task-specific heading + Lead with when to use it + Contact pathway + Hours and location or response expectation.

Use when users need staff help, subject expertise, accommodation, follow-up, or escalation. Prefer the most accurate pathway for the task over a generic “Contact Us” tile. For research support, identify the kind of help, such as choosing sources, citing, data management, or archives access.

Do not publish individual staff names as the primary support path unless that is the established and maintained public pathway.

### Form

**Composition:** Form title + Lead + Field labels + Helper text + Validation and error messages + Submit call to action.

Use for requests, registrations, nominations, feedback, and appointments. Label fields by the information needed, keep helper text near the field, and explain how to recover from errors. The submit label should describe the result, such as “Send request” or “Submit nomination.”

Do not replace a visible label with placeholder text. Preserve required field status and any privacy or eligibility condition without burying it in helper text.

### Confirmation or follow-up

**Composition:** Result title + What happened + What comes next + Expected timeframe or status path + Help path if needed.

Use after submission, account renewal, request placement, reservation, or successful sign-in. Avoid congratulatory language that delays the user’s next step. Put the next action before any marketing or related-service content.

Example:

- **Request sent.** We’ll email you when your item is ready for pickup. Check your Library account for status.

### Catalog and resource action

**Composition:** Resource title + Format, availability, or location + Action call to action + Access guidance or policy link.

Use for search results, item records, databases, journals, datasets, media, and course reserves. Make the state clear before the user selects an action: available, checked out, online, onsite use only, or available through interlibrary loan. Use exact system names such as UC Library Search, Get it at UC, and the UC San Diego Library catalog.

Avoid assuming that all users know which catalog, sign-in, or pickup workflow applies to them.

### Location and wayfinding

**Composition:** Place name + Floor or level + Direction or nearby landmark + Hours or availability + Accessibility or access detail when relevant.

Use for desks, reading rooms, classrooms, collections, restrooms, and service points. Use exact Library names, such as Geisel Library Front Desk and WongAvery Library Front Desk. Give the route from a recognizable entry point rather than relying on room codes alone.

Do not use an image or map as the sole source of directions.

### Event or promotion

**Composition:** Event or promotion title + Lead or hook + Date and time + Location or online format + Audience + Registration or action call to action.

Use for workshops, exhibitions, orientations, deadlines, prize nominations, and featured resources. Keep the hook meaningful and brief; the essential action and facts must be immediately visible. Include deadlines, eligibility, and time zone when relevant.

Use promotional language to invite participation, not to obscure the task or overstate the Library’s services.

### Related task or resource

**Composition:** Task label + Short description or destination name + Link.

Use to help users recover from a mismatch or move to the next likely task, such as from “Borrow and request” to course reserves, technology lending, research consultations, or visitor access. Curate the two or three most useful pathways rather than listing all services.

Make the distinction clear when two services sound similar.

### Image and icon support

**Composition:** Image or icon + Text label or title + Alt text + Caption or helper text as needed.

Use visuals to support recognition and orientation. Write alt text for the meaning the image communicates, not a list of pixels. If the image is decorative, it should not add information the user must process. Pair every functional icon with a clear text label.

## Drafting Checklist

For each service page or molecule:

1. Name the user’s primary task and the approved service or offering.
2. Lead with the action or benefit before process, policy, or background.
3. Preserve eligibility, limits, deadlines, costs, and exceptions.
4. State the next step in the button, link, form label, or confirmation.
5. Use exact service, location, system, and collection names.
6. Add only the details needed for the user to decide and act.
7. Make help, alternatives, and recovery paths visible where users can fail.
8. Check that the molecule works without surrounding context.
9. Route final copy for human expert review and any needed policy, accessibility, or user-testing review.
