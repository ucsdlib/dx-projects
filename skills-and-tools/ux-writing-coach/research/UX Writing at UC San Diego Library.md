# UX Writing at UC San Diego Library

## Campus voice, Library voice and AP Style

All Library UX writing should feel like UC San Diego, sound like the Library and follow a shared editorial rule set. Effective Library UX writing brings together three standards that shape that work: the UC San Diego campus brand voice, the Library’s own voice and AP/UC San Diego web editorial style. Use all three together whenever you write for Library UX.  

### UC San Diego brand voice

The UC San Diego brand is built on a two-word internal compass: "Curiosity Must." It's not outward-facing copy, but it should live in everything you write. 

When writing for Library UX, ask yourself: *does this feel curious? Does this feel driven?*

The brand has five tone qualities. Dial them up or down based on context, but all five should be present in any piece of copy.

- **Courageous**: Confident without being institutional
- **Sincere**: Honest and human; never performative
- **Consequential**: What we do connects to something real
- **Agile**: Forward-moving; no bureaucratic drag
- **Collective**: We're a community

The UC San Diego brand is human. Use "you" and "we." Use contractions. Take positions.

**Essential:** Write UC San Diego (never UCSD)

- <https://univcomms.ucsd.edu/resources/editorial-style-guide/index.html>

### Library voice

The UC San Diego brand is bold and forward-driven. The Library is all of that, and we are also **welcoming, inclusive and helpful.**

Libraries carry a historical reputation for being intimidating and exclusive, especially for students who are newer to academic research or who have been made to feel that scholarly spaces aren't for them. Countering that reputation with warmth, curiosity, and genuine welcome is essential in our UX writing.

Library web copy lives at the intersection of four qualities:

- **Warm.** Write to a person, not a user profile. Contractions are good. The Library is approachable, not formal.
- **Inviting.** Every page is an open door. Lead with what users can do before you list the rules or requirements they'll need to meet. "Here's how to get started" before "here's what's required."
- **Inclusive.** A first-generation student and a fourth-year doctoral researcher may land on the same page. Assume nothing about a reader's prior experience, confidence, or familiarity with library systems. Write so both feel seen.
- **Curious.** Questions are welcome here. Not knowing where to start is welcome here. Copy should signal that the Library is genuinely interested in what users are working on.

When writing for Library UX, ask yourself: *does this feel inclusive? Does this feel helpful? Would a student who has never used an academic library feel welcomed or overwhelmed by this copy?*

### AP Stylebook and UC San Diego web editorial style

All UC San Diego web copy follows the [AP Stylebook](http://www.apstylebook.com/) as interpreted by the [UC San Diego Web Editorial Style Guide](https://blink.ucsd.edu/go/styleguide). The Library has access to the [AP Stylebook online through the Library](https://go.openathens.net/redirector/ucsd.edu?url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.apstylebook.com%2Fuc-san-diego).

**The AP style rules you'll use most often:**

- Numbers: spell out one through nine; use numerals for 10 and above. Always use numerals for ages, percentages and times.
- Capitalization: minimize it. Capitalize proper nouns and formal titles before names ("Chancellor Pradeep Khosla"), but not generic terms like "database" or "research guide" unless they're part of a formal name. Capitalize “Library” or “the Library” when referring to UC San Diego Library, but do not capitalize “library” when using as a generic term.
- Time: "8 a.m.–5 p.m." not "8:00 AM to 5:00 PM"
- Dates: abbreviate months with six or more letters when used with a specific date. "The workshop runs Jan. 15." Spell out March, April, May, June and July.
- Contractions: encouraged. "You'll find," "We're open," "Don't miss it."
- Oxford comma: AP style omits it in a simple series. "Books, articles and databases." Add it only when the last two items could be misread as a pair.
- Gender-neutral pronouns: use "they/them" as singular. "A student can pick up their holds" not "his or her holds."
- Headings: sentence case. Capitalize only the first word and proper nouns.

**Em dashes:** Avoid them. Use commas, periods, colons or semicolons instead. (This is both an AP preference and an AI writing tell.)

## How people read on the web

Before you write a word, know that most users don't read web pages. They scan. Research from Nielsen Norman Group found that about 79% of users scan any new page they visit; only 16% read word by word. On average, users read roughly 20% of the words on a page.

This doesn't mean writing quality doesn't matter. It means copy has to work harder to be read at all.

**Scannable copy requires **writing in the inverted pyramid: lead with the conclusion, then add supporting detail, then background. Don't build to a point. Start with it.

One idea per paragraph. If a paragraph contains two ideas, split it.

Make sure headings and sub-headings are meaningful. "Request a book from another library" carries a clear meaning that helps users decide whether to read the section. "Getting what you need" makes users guess.

Aim for half the word count of conventional writing, or less. Every sentence should earn its place.

**NNG evidence (Nielsen, 1997 usability study):**  
Concise text improved usability by 58% over promotional writing. Scannable layout improved usability by 47%. Objective language improved usability by 27%. All three together: 124% improvement.

Full reference: [nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)

## Copy types: Long-form, Short-form and Microcopy

Every piece of Library copy falls into one of three categories. Identify which one you're writing before you start.

**Long-form** (3 or more paragraphs that form a continuous unit). Use for policy pages, service documentation, longer how-to guides, "About" copy, and anything requiring complex detail. Lead with the most important information; add context last.

**Short-form** (1–3 paragraphs, a small number of main ideas). Use for service descriptions, help articles, feature introductions and onboarding content. Short enough to scan; complete enough to give users what they need without reading long-form copy.

**Microcopy** (fewer than 3 sentences). Use for labels, button text, error messages, tooltips, navigation, placeholder text and empty states. The smallest copy type and the most-read. Every word counts.

Full reference: [nngroup.com/articles/ux-copy-sizes](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-copy-sizes/)

### Microcopy: the essentials

Every piece of microcopy has one primary goal. Name it before you write.

- **Inform**: deliver information or guidance (tooltip text, error explanations, placeholder instructions)
- **Influence**:  encourage a specific action (CTAs, promotional copy, taglines)  
- **Interact**: support interface use (button labels, navigation, breadcrumbs, form field labels)

**The most common microcopy mistakes:**

- Buttons that say "Submit" instead of what actually happens. ("Send request" is better. "Request this item" is better still.)
- Error messages that state a status without offering a path forward. ("This item isn't in right now. We can get it from another library, usually within a few days." is better than "Item unavailable.")
- "Click here" as link text. Describe the destination. ("See borrowing policies" not "Click here for more information.")
- "Learn more" as a CTA. Learn more about *what*? 
- Restrictions framed as barriers. ("Sign in with your UC San Diego credentials to access this resource" is a door. "Access restricted" is a wall.)

Full reference: [nngroup.com/articles/3-is-of-microcopy](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/3-is-of-microcopy/)

## Handling jargon

Library systems are full of terms that librarians know well and most users have never encountered. Before using any specialized term, ask two questions.

**Will most readers know this term?** If yes, use it without explanation. If no, ask the second question.

**How important is it to use this specific term?** If users will need the term to search, if it's used elsewhere in the interface, or if precision requires it then use both the plain-language version and the term together. "Borrow books from other libraries (interlibrary loan)" on a page for first-year undergraduates. If the term doesn't matter, replace it with plain language and move on.

Always write out acronyms on first reference: "interlibrary loan (ILL)". Consider not using acronyms at all when possible.

### Defining terminology examples

| Term | How to introduce and define |
| --- | --- |
| Interlibrary loan | "borrow books from other libraries (interlibrary loan)" |
| Peer-reviewed | "articles reviewed by other experts in the field" |
| Citation management | "tools to organize your sources" |
| Metadata | "record details like title, author and subject" |
| Federated search | Avoid; describe the behavior instead |

Full reference: [nngroup.com/articles/technical-jargon](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/technical-jargon/)

## Brevity techniques

Scannability requires concision. These six techniques will cut most drafts by 30–40% without losing meaning.

- **Focus on essentials for users.** The phrases you love most are often the ones users need least. Ask: is this necessary for the reader to understand the point? If not, cut it.
- **Remove filler.** "It is important to remember that..." just delays the sentence. Start with the thing itself.
- **Cut unnecessary adverbs.** "Very," "extremely," "quite" and "really" weaken sentences rather than strengthen them. Remove them.
- **Remove redundancy.** 

**One idea, one sentence.** If a sentence contains more than one main idea, it probably needs to become two sentences.

Full reference: [nngroup.com/articles/rewriting-content-brevity](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/rewriting-content-brevity/)

## Words that sound like AI

These patterns mark writing as algorithmically generated. They also tend to be imprecise and wordy. Avoid all of them.

**Structure patterns to remove:**

- Em dashes (“—”). Use commas, periods, colons or semicolons instead.
- "Not only X, but also Y." Instead, state things directly.
- "X isn't just about Y." Say what it is, not what it isn't

**Words and phrases to cut:**

- leverage, utilize, empower, unlock, enable (use concrete descriptions)
- revolutionary, game-changing, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art
- "In today's rapidly changing world" or any variant
- "It could be argued," "one might consider," "it is possible that"
- "It is important to note that," "it's worth mentioning that"
- very, really, quite, fairly, somewhat (as intensifiers)
- "Let me explain," "let's explore," "let's break this down"
- "Remember," "ultimately," "at the end of the day" (as closers)

**Replace with:** specific descriptions, direct statements, concrete numbers and named examples.

## The draft checklist

Before delivering any copy, run through this list.

* [ ] **Warm and inclusive**: would a first-year student feel welcomed, not overwhelmed?
* [ ] **Sounds like UC San Diego**: curious, human, courageous and consequential
* [ ] **AP and UC San Diego style**: "UC San Diego," correct capitalization, right numerals, sentence-case headings
* [ ] **Scannable**: inverted pyramid, one idea per paragraph, active voice
* [ ] **Free of AI tells**: no em dashes, no hedging, no hype language
