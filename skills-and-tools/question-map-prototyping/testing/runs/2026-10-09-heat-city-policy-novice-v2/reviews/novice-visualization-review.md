# Novice Visualization Review

## Verdict

**Conditional pass.** The map is understandable and useful once I found the linear reading view, but the graph view is visually dense and the link meanings took some work to interpret. The core structure is strong, and it avoids several mistakes I might have expected from a broad policy question.

## Difficulty

**3 / 5.** The map is not hard to read conceptually, but the visualization is busy and the graph labels are small. The linear reading view made the material much easier for me.

## What I Could Do

### Main research question

The main question is: **What should the city do about extreme heat and public health, using recent research evidence for a city council meeting?**

I liked that this broad prompt stayed as the inquiry frame rather than being turned into a narrower claim that no retrieved source actually answers.

### Main themes

I found five main themes:

1. Heat action plans and governance
2. Intervention evidence and trade-offs
3. Heat and health outcomes
4. Heat plan evaluation methods
5. Heat risk distribution

These felt understandable as a non-expert, though “governance” took a moment to interpret as city planning, coordination, and policy action.

### One source supporting one question

The source **Randazza et al., 2023** directly supports the question **“What components do existing heat action plans include and omit to reduce adverse health effects?”**

The linked source question is stated as: “What commonalities and gaps exist in local U.S. heat action plans designed to decrease adverse health effects of extreme heat?” That maps closely to the broader question about what plans include and omit.

I also noticed that this appears to be abstract/metadata support, not full-text verification, so I would not treat it as a final evidence conclusion.

### Stated versus inferred questions

I could distinguish these:

- **Stated** means the source itself asks the question, such as the Randazza example above.
- **Inferred** means the tool has derived or generalized a source question, such as “How should public health systems prepare for extreme heat events?”

The labeling was helpful, but a first-time reader may need a one-line reminder on the graph itself explaining what “Inferred” means.

### Direct support versus cross-cutting relevance

**Direct support** is a solid connection from a question to a source that explicitly addresses that question. For example, Randazza et al. directly supports the heat-plan-components question.

**Cross-cutting relevance** is different: it shows that one theme is conceptually relevant to a question that belongs to another theme. For example, “Heat and health outcomes” cross-cuts the infrastructure question, but that does not mean the health-outcomes theme directly supplies evidence that a particular infrastructure strategy works.

This distinction is important and mostly clear once explained.

### Relationship filter

The interactive view has a filter with options for direct support, secondary links, cross-cutting links, and possible next questions. That is the right design. However, I initially was not sure what changed when I switched the filter because some dashed lines look similar.

The filter would be easier to use if each selected relationship type also showed a short explanation, such as “Direct support: source explicitly addresses this question.”

### One possible next question

I would choose: **“Which neighborhoods and groups in the user’s city experience the greatest heat-health burden and least access to cooling?”**

This feels directly useful for a council discussion because it connects heat risk, health outcomes, and equitable access.

## What I Would Do Next

I would narrow the inquiry to the city’s own neighborhoods and vulnerable populations, then ask for a focused annotated bibliography on local heat-health burden and cooling access. I would also look for local data or plans, since the map notes that no specific city was named.

## Confusing, Unreadable, or Misleading Items

- The graph is visually dense and could overwhelm someone unfamiliar with research mapping.
- Dashed line styles are not immediately distinguishable in the graph view.
- “Direct source-question support” is useful but jargon-like; a plain-language explanation would help.
- Some source cards contain long titles and long link labels, making the graph harder to scan.
- The possible-next-question section is clear that these are not source-backed findings, which is good.
- The “Not found” and “Limitations” sections are especially helpful for preventing overclaiming.

## Overall Novice Impression

This is a strong first-pass research map. It made the broad city-policy question manageable, kept relevant evidence categories separate, and offered useful next steps. With clearer line semantics and a less crowded graph view, it would be easier for a novice city staff member or resident to use confidently.
