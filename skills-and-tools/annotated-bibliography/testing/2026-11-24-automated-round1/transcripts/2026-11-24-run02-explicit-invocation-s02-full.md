> ## Overviews
>
> - [Khodjaev et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.30935/ojcmt/18954): meta-analysis of 62 studies, total N = 17,800, reporting a moderate negative association between social media addiction and psychological well-being, with high heterogeneity.
> - [Ridout and Campbell (2018)](https://doi.org/10.2196/12244): systematic review of social networking site-based mental health interventions for young people, finding promising engagement and support but limited high-quality efficacy evidence.
> - [Plackett et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.2196/44922): systematic review of 23 social media use interventions, finding some well-being benefits, especially for depression and therapy-based approaches, but generally weak study quality.
>
> ## Themes
>
> 1. **Problematic and addictive use:** Recurring risk cluster linking problematic use to depression, anxiety, distress, and poorer well-being, though causality remains uneven across designs.
> 2. **Use type matters:** Passive scrolling, active interaction, interaction valence, impression management, and late-night use behave differently from raw time online.
> 3. **Comparison and appearance:** Social media comparison connects to depression, anxiety, appearance anxiety, body shame, and related well-being outcomes.
> 4. **Sleep and fatigue pathways:** Social media use, addiction, fatigue, insomnia, and academic strain often appear in the same explanatory chain.
> 5. **Disclosure and peer support:** Students may recognize mental health distress in peers online, but stigma, uncertainty, and emotional distance can inhibit support.
> 6. **Detox and intervention evidence:** Reduction, abstinence, and therapy-based interventions show some promise, but methodological quality and outcomes vary.

## Turn 3 — User

> Which of these sources should I be most skeptical about, and why?

## Turn 3 — Skill

> I'd be most skeptical, in this order:
>
> 1. **Khodjaev et al. (2026)** — It's the broadest source, but that's also why I'd be careful. It reports a strong pooled association, yet the heterogeneity is high, and the meta-analysis appears in a communication/media technologies journal rather than a psychology or public-health venue. That doesn't make it wrong, but I'd want to see how the authors handled study quality and whether "addiction" was defined consistently across studies.
>
> 2. **Plackett et al. (2023)** — The systematic review is useful, but it explicitly notes weak study quality and lots of convenience samples. So I'd trust it more as "some evidence that interventions can help" than as proof of a specific intervention effect.
>
> 3. **Ojha et al. (2021)** — Small cross-sectional study, narrow population, and a conclusion that itself calls the relationship "complex." It's a good source for framing social comparison, but I wouldn't lean on it as strong causal evidence.
>
> 4. **Sahib et al. (2026)** — Interesting and methodologically relevant because it looks at interaction quality rather than just time online, but the sample is small. I'd treat it as a useful conceptual lead, not as a reliable estimate of effect size.
>
> 5. **Luqman et al. (2020)** — Large sample, but the causal chain from late-night use to sleep to cognition to academic performance is more inferential than the abstract alone can support. I'd be cautious about using it as a direct mental-health mechanism.
>
> The broad caveat is that all of these were annotated at abstract level, so none should be treated as fully verified yet. If you want one first deep read, I'd start with **Khodjaev et al.** because its headline effect is the most influential, and then **Plackett et al.** because it shapes the intervention argument.
