> Which of these sources should I be most skeptical about, and why?

## Turn 3 — Skill

> Good question — and the honest answer is that skepticism shouldn't be spread evenly. A few sources carry a lot of interpretive weight relative to what their designs can actually support.
>
> **Most skeptical: the cross-sectional "mediation" studies** — Zuo & Zan (2025), Tung et al. (2025), Chentsova et al. (2023), and Li et al.'s network analysis (2022). These are the sources that *name mechanisms*, which makes them tempting to lean on, but their designs can't support causal language. When Zuo & Zan say upward comparison "reduces well-being through" lowered self-esteem, that's a modeled pathway measured at one point in time; it's equally consistent with lower well-being making students more prone to comparison. Chentsova's rumination findings are the strongest of the four (8,912 participants, seven countries, measurement invariance tested), but "mediates" still describes a cross-sectional model, not a demonstrated causal chain. If your capstone cites these for *what causes what*, that's where you'd be overclaiming.
>
> **Skeptical in a different way: the two experiments.** Hunt et al. (2018) has the best design in the set, but note what the abstract itself concedes: both conditions improved on anxiety and FoMO, which means self-monitoring — not just restriction — may explain part of the effect. And it's 143 students at one university over three weeks. Thai et al. (2021) is even more fragile: N = 38, and appearance esteem and anxiety improved while weight esteem and depression didn't. Both are valuable *because* they're experiments, but neither supports a broad "social media causes depression" claim — Hunt's own conclusion is carefully scoped to ~30 minutes/day.
>
> **Skeptical about transfer: Marino et al. (2018) and the two critique reviews.** Marino's r = .34 is the field's anchor effect size, but it's for *problematic Facebook use* specifically, pooled across adolescents and young adults, and every included study was cross-sectional. Orben (2020) and Odgers & Jensen (2020) are rigorous, but they're adolescent-focused — applying their "very small effects" conclusion to college students is a reasonable inference, not a demonstrated one.
>
> **Skeptical about the cyberbullying strand.** Martínez-Monteagudo et al. (2020) links cybervictimization to suicidal thinking in a large sample, but the direction is unknowable from a cross-sectional design — distressed students may also be more likely to be victimized, or the two may share a third cause. Bussu et al. (2025) is a solid synthesis but narrative, so no pooled effect sizes, and it explicitly reports that intervention evidence is thin.
>
> **One practical note:** all 16 entries were built from abstracts, so none of these caveats come from full texts. The bibliography's "verification targets" section flags exactly which claims in Hunt, Marino, Li (sleep), Zhou, and Orben most need full-text confirmation before you rely on them.
>
> If I had to compress it: be most skeptical wherever a source uses causal verbs ("reduces," "increases," "improves") on cross-sectional data, and most trusting — with scope caveats — of the randomized designs. That distinction is itself a defensible theme for your capstone.
