> ## Themes
>
> The retrieved studies cluster into six themes, and each suggests a different way you could frame a capstone.
>
> ### Time online versus how you use it
>
> The strongest pattern across reviews and meta-analyses is that *time spent* on social media shows weak associations with depression and anxiety, while *problematic or addictive* use shows moderate associations. One experience-sampling study of 190 university students even found no association between social networking use and clinical depression.
>
> **Questions explored in this theme:** Is more time on social media associated with worse depression or anxiety, or does problematic use matter more? Does the way students use platforms matter more than how long they use them?
>
> **Representative readings:**
>
> - [Social Media and Depression Symptoms: A Meta-Analysis, Cunningham et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10802-020-00715-7) — Finds time spent weakly associated with depression symptoms (r = 0.11) while problematic use is moderately associated (r = 0.29).
> - [The Associations Between Problematic Facebook Use, Psychological Distress and Well-Being, Marino et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2017.10.007) — Meta-analysis of 23 samples finding problematic Facebook use positively correlated with psychological distress (r = 0.34).
> - ["Facebook Depression?" Social Networking Site Use and Depression in Older Adolescents, Jelenchick et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2012.05.008) — Small experience-sampling study of university students finding no evidence linking SNS use to clinical depression.

> ## Possible directions
>
> These are openings suggested by patterns and gaps in the retrieved sources, framed as possibilities for your capstone rather than claims about the field.
>
> - **Mechanisms over screen time** — The retrieved meta-analyses suggest time alone explains little, which opens room to study *how* students use platforms: passive browsing, comparison, envy, or fear of missing out.
> - **Sleep as a mediator** — Several studies link social media use to sleep problems and then to downstream risk, suggesting a testable pathway between use patterns and mental-health outcomes.
> - **Measurement as a research question** — Studies using "time spent" versus "problematic use" reach different conclusions, which could ground a capstone comparing measures or arguing for a specific construct.
> - **Replication in your population** — Many retrieved samples come from China, Switzerland, Peru, or Ethiopia, and studies with U.S. college samples are smaller or more scattered, so a replication in a specific student population could fill a gap.
> - **Intervention evaluation** — Reviews report that therapy-based approaches outperform abstinence, but study quality is rated weak, suggesting room for a stronger study design.

>
> **What to double-check yourself:**
>
> 1. **Read the full texts, not just my abstract-level summaries.** Every source in this list was screened at the abstract level, so effect sizes, exact wording of findings, and stated limitations all need confirming in the actual papers before you cite them.
> 2. **Scrutinize the causal language.** Most of these are cross-sectional. Even Li et al.'s longitudinal paths include one that was only "marginally significant," which is a fragile result. Check the exact p-values and whether other longitudinal studies replicate that direction.
> 3. **Check what "use" actually means in each paper.** Leow's meta-analysis uses *smartphone* addiction scales, not social-media-specific ones. Cui et al. measure *mobile phone* use. If your capstone is specifically about social media, confirm the construct in each source matches, or note the mismatch explicitly.
> 4. **Check who was studied.** Samples come from China, Peru, Brazil, Ethiopia, Switzerland, and the US, and several are medical students specifically. Ask whether findings generalize to your population, and flag it as a limitation if they don't.
> 5. **Verify the anxiety claim yourself.** My reading list documents sleep mediating links to depression, fatigue, and suicidal ideation, but anxiety-specific longitudinal evidence was thin in what I retrieved. If anxiety is your angle, you'll need to search for that gap directly rather than assume this set covers it.
> 6. **Check confounds.** Look at what each study adjusted for — age, sex, BMI, lifestyle in Olivo et al., for instance — and ask what was *not* controlled that could explain the association instead.
