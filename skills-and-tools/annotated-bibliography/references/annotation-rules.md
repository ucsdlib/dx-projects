# Annotation Rules Reference

Use this reference for full annotated bibliographies, confidence judgments, weak-evidence reporting, and closing next steps.

## Annotation Structure

For each source:

```markdown
# [lowercase descriptive title of key finding]

---
**Citation:** [Full APA 7th edition with DOI or URL]
**Source ID:** [Stable run-state ID]
**Access Level:** [Full text reviewed / Abstract only / Publisher description and TOC reviewed (via Primo record) / Metadata only]
**Source Type:** [Empirical study / Review / Theoretical / Practitioner / Book]
**Related Themes:** [Theme IDs]
**Related Questions:** [Question IDs]
---

## What the Source Claims

[Two to four paragraphs staying close to the source’s explicit statements. Use direct quotes for key claims.]

**Key quotes:**
- “[Direct quote one]”
- “[Direct quote two]”

## Methodology/Evidence

**Study Design:** [Type]
**Evidence Base:** [Sample, data sources, methods, limitations]
**Credibility:** [Peer-reviewed? Citations? Author expertise? Scholarly press? Limitations acknowledged?]

## Relevance to Research Question

**Research Question:** [Restate for clarity]

### Connection Classification

**1. Direct Evidence** (if applicable):  
Source explicitly discusses [topic] and demonstrates [finding] through [evidence].  
**Quote:** “[supporting quote]”

**2. Reasonable Inference** (if applicable):  
Source demonstrates [related finding], suggesting [connection] because [reasoning].

**3. Interpretive Connection** (if applicable):  
Source addresses [related domain], which could be relevant because [reasoning]. However, this requires [describe leap].

## Confidence Level: [HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW]

**Justification:** [Explain rating based on directness, rigor, generalizability, and inference required.]

---
```

## Organization

The opening summary should include:

- Research question.
- Breadth tier and its basis.
- Search strategy.
- A pipeline-transparency line naming tools and notable coverage gaps.
- Scope constraints.
- Key findings, debate, or gaps.
- Structure of the bibliography.

Useful section headers include core empirical evidence, theoretical frameworks, contrasting perspectives or critiques, methodological studies, applied or practitioner work, and historical or foundational sources.

The closing synthesis should address consensus areas, contested issues, methodological notes, gaps, unexpected findings, and recommendations.

## Human Next Steps

Every bibliography closes with human next steps. The bibliography orients; the human investigates.

Required components:

1. **Priority reading list (three to six sources):** sources that most reward full-text human reading, each with a one-line reason. Prioritize sources whose claims carry the most weight, those accessed only at abstract or description level, and those requiring authenticated retrieval.
2. **Verification targets:** specific claims, statistics, or quotes where abstract-level access or inference does heavy lifting. Phrase each as a question the human can answer by reading.
3. **Open questions for the researcher (two to four):** compelling questions surfaced by the search but not answered by available evidence. Frame them as research directions, not deficiencies.
4. **Targeted follow-up searches (two or three):** concrete queries, databases, or citation-chaining leads tied to documented gaps.
5. **Reading-and-verification guidance:** state that the human should select the most relevant sources, obtain and read full texts, verify key claims, and reach their own conclusions. Annotations are screening and orientation, not substitutes for source engagement. Where a source disagrees with its annotation, the source wins.

Every item must trace to a named source, logged gap, or contested finding. If the evidence base is weak, say what reading would strengthen it and why.

## Confidence Guidelines

**HIGH:** source directly addresses the research question through rigorous empirical methods; no interpretive leap is required.

**MEDIUM:** source provides solid evidence but requires modest inference, addresses a closely related domain, or is verified only at publisher-description and table-of-contents level.

**LOW:** source requires substantial interpretive work across periods, disciplines, or levels of analysis, or is available only at metadata level.

## Guardrails

Before finalizing an annotation, check:

- Did I add interpretation not in the source? Move it to “Interpretive Connection.”
- Did I smooth over contradictions? Include them.
- Did I change the source’s terminology without justification? Justify or acknowledge the shift.
- Did I quote accurately and in context? Fix any error immediately.
- Did I make the source fit the user’s hypothesis? Describe what the source actually says.
- Did I inflate confidence? Lower it and explain honestly.

## Weak or Absent Evidence

If the literature reveals limited evidence:

- Say so clearly.
- Do not force tangential sources to count.
- Acknowledge gaps explicitly.
- Note what kind of evidence is missing.

Common patterns:

- **No contrasting evidence found:** report strong support, then note that absence of challenges may reflect search limitations, consensus, or lack of scholarly attention.
- **Popular claims versus academic evidence:** include practitioner advocacy and academic critiques; report divergence honestly.
- **No full text:** mark the access level, lower confidence, and focus on what the abstract or description explicitly states.

## Final Reminders

- Accuracy is not advocacy.
- Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
- Quote liberally as evidence for faithful representation.
- Access level matters.
- When in doubt, rate confidence lower.
- A bibliography that contradicts the user’s hypothesis can be the most valuable result.
- Gaps and contradictions are findings.
